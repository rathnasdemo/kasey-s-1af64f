> **TL;DR:** This is the first lesson in my beginner recorder series. I cover the parts of the recorder, how to assemble it, and how the tone holes are numbered. Then I show how to hold it with the left thumb on hole zero and the index finger on hole one. You don't need much air, so if it sounds shrill you're blowing too hard. Breathe low, cover the holes completely, learn the notes B, A and G from finger diagrams, and play Hot Cross Buns.

Welcome to this beginner recorder lesson, the first video in this series. We're going to learn the parts of the recorder, how to hold it, and those cool things called finger diagrams. We'll also learn to play a tune or two, specifically with the notes B, A and G.

I have my soprano recorder here. It's nothing fancy, just something I bought off Amazon. I spray painted it neon orange so that it glows in black light or UV light.

## The parts of the recorder

*[Watch from 0:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=41s)*

When you get your recorder, it might be disassembled into three pieces.

- **Head joint.** The top part, with the mouthpiece on top. This is the part we blow into. It connects into the body, and when you assemble it, make sure the window lines up with the finger holes on the front.
- **Body.** The middle piece with the finger holes.
- **Foot joint.** The bottom part. Align it so the holes are in line, but turn it back a little, so it looks turned slightly to the left and the holes are off-center. The reason is that our pinky is shorter than the rest of our fingers and we have to compensate for that.

## Numbering the holes

*[Watch from 1:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=96s)*

The finger holes, or tone holes, all have assigned numbers. There's also one on the back that our thumb usually covers, and that's hole zero. Then it goes one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, and towards the bottom we actually get two holes. It's helpful to know these numbers because the finger diagrams that tell us how to play notes and songs refer to each hole by number.

## How to hold the recorder

*[Watch from 2:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=125s)*

Holding the recorder is pretty simple. We mainly use our left hand. Use your thumb to cover the hole on the back, hole number zero, and your index finger, or pointer finger, to cover the first hole on top. That's all we need to hold it, and you could actually play it just like that. Covering zero and the first hole gives the note B.

It feels more stable if we bring the right hand down, with the little thumb rest going on top of the right thumb so the recorder sits on it. If we're not pressing any holes with the right hand, we can rest our fingertips against the body of the recorder, off to the side and not covering any holes, for extra support.

## Finger diagrams and the note B

*[Watch from 3:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=180s)*

Finger diagrams are super helpful to understand because they teach us how to play different notes. In the diagram for B, the solid, filled-in holes are the ones we cover with our fingers. Hole zero is filled in, which is our thumb on the back, and the first tone hole is also covered, by our pointer finger on the front.

## How much air you need

*[Watch from 3:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=222s)*

I purposely played that last note too hard so you could hear how harsh it sounded. We don't really need a lot of air to play the notes on the recorder. If it sounds harsh, loud or really shrill, you're probably blowing too hard. Keep in mind the people around you, your family, your friends or your pets. It is very ear-piercing, and I guarantee you will send small animals running in all directions.

I once heard a teacher say that you only need enough air from one nostril to play the recorder. I was teaching fourth grade students at the time, I told them this, and guess what they did. They had to see if it worked.

Then we played the B again, three times in a row.

## The note A

*[Watch from 4:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=290s)*

In the finger diagram for A, we have zero and one covered, and now two. If we're playing B and want to go to A, we just add our middle finger on the second tone hole.

If you get a note that sounds wrong, it probably means one of the holes isn't being covered all the way. If I slightly move my thumb on the back while playing, you'll hear the notes keep changing. Make sure the holes are completely covered to get the right note.

## Breathing

*[Watch from 5:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=345s)*

The way we breathe is important. We want to support our breath by breathing low. If you take a deep breath in, you want to feel your stomach poof out, because that means your diaphragm, the muscle that holds up your lungs, is relaxing down. You get the most supportive air from this part of the body. When we breathe up high, we have very shallow, unsupported breaths.

It's a good thing to do in life in general to breathe deeply. It means you have a relaxed diaphragm, and it can help with anxiety, nervousness and just being calm.

Then we went back to B and played it three times, and did the same with A.

## The note G

*[Watch from 8:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=481s)*

Let's learn one more note for today: G. To play G, we cover zero, one, two and three. On the finger diagram for G, we see those four holes covered, and then a line below them, which marks where the left hand ends and the right hand begins. So we're using everything but the pinky on the left hand.

Then we played the notes B, A, G, and did it again.

## Hot Cross Buns

*[Watch from 8:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=72D2638uS1E&t=535s)*

Now let's do the classic song Hot Cross Buns. We go B, A, G, B, A, G. Then we do four G's and four A's, and then B, A, G again. Finally we played the whole song with the notes on the screen.
